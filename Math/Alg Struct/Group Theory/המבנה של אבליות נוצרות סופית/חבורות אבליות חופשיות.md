>Def (7.6) (Basis of abelian group):
>	Let $A$ be an abelian group. And $\mathcal{S} = \left\{ \alpha_i \right\}_{i \in I} \subseteq A$. Then $\mathcal{S}$ is called basis of $A$ when for all $a \in A$, a can be represented uniquely as a finite sum
>	$$a = \sum\limits_{i} m_i\alpha_i$$
>	for $m_i \in \mathbb{Z}$

^581050

>Note:
>	>$I$ does not have to be finite, but every $a$ should be a finite sum.

>	>The definition of basis here, for abelian groups, and the definition of basis for general groups we will see later [[]], are not aligned. Here changing the order does not matter, there it would matter a lot. So $a = b + c = c + b$ is considered one representation here, and there it would be considered two representations.
>	
>	>The definition of basis here (of abelian gorups) is very similar to the basis of a vector space. The main difference is that when all vector spaces have a basis, not all abelian groups do.

>Def (7.9) (Free abelian group):
>	An abelian group that has a basis (according to [[#^581050 |the abelian definition of a basis]]) is called "a free abelian" group.

>Theorem (7.8) (Free abelian finitely generated group is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}^r$):
>	Let $A$ be a free abelian group, with a finite basis of size $r \in \mathbb{N}$. Then $A \cong \mathbb{Z}^r$.
>Proof:
> 	Let $\mathcal{S} = \left\{ \alpha_i \right\}_{i \in [r]}$ be a basis for $A$. We have $\varphi:\mathbb{Z}^r \to A$ $(c_1, \dots , c_r) \mapsto \sum\limits_{i=1}^{r} c_i\alpha_i$. It is clearly an homomorphism, and [[#^581050 |by the definition of a basis]] it is clearly onto and one-to-one (one to one for example because only $(0, \dots , 0)$ maps to $0_A$ for unique representation, and because of [[]] (a known fact about one to one homomorphisms)).
> 	So $\varphi$ is a isomorphism. $\blacksquare$
